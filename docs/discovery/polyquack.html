How to read this book

Each chapter answers one question. The blue box at the start is the short answer: if you read only those boxes, you already have the book. Every claim carries its level of evidence:

  • Confirmed the data or the text of a primary source says so.
  • Hypothesis deduced, still to be confirmed.
  • Ask only you can decide.
  • Issue broken, incomplete or conflicting data.

The book on one page

In a market with several options, buying all of them should cost 1 USD, because in the end exactly one pays 1 USD. Sometimes the sum is not 1, but what counts is the price you can really buy at, not the one the page shows. Taking the difference costs: the fee, the available volume, and the time the money stays trapped.

A 2026 paper already measured these differences with real prices. The ones that close last seconds and cannot be reached from home. The ones that last hours are almost always collected by waiting for settlement. Ladders (markets that only change a threshold or a date) are detected with a very precise rule, but in one snapshot they yielded about 17 USD in total. The public weather forecast is already priced in.

The prediction-market angles did not demonstrate net profit. The one that came closest came from another Polymarket product, its perpetual futures (Perps). Their funding rate separates systematically from that of another platform, and a simple strategy left out-of-sample profit, though with only two months of history. Chapter 9 compares the angles against your criteria and chapter 10 lists what is yours to decide.

The angles

Part II reviews six ways to look for an edge. Letters A to D come from the ideation session, E from your question after reading the first version, and calibration was there since the brief.

  • A · Speed: take price differences before everyone else.
  • B · Ladders: find markets whose prices break a logical rule.
  • C · Weather: forecast temperature better than the market.
  • D · Crypto options: derive a fair price from another, more professional market.
  • Calibration: exploit a systematic error made by the people who trade.
  • E · Perps: collect the difference in funding rate between Polymarket and another platform.

Keys such as CR-001 or DQ-004 are the names of your criteria and questions in findings.md; the book always says what they mean.

Part I · The terrain

1Why don't prices sometimes add up to 1?

Short answer In a market with several options, exactly one pays 1 USD in the end, so buying them all should cost 1. But what you see on the page is not what you pay: what counts is the price you can really buy at in the order book. A displayed price that adds up to more than 1 is usually the normal margin, not an opportunity.

Case

On Oct 26, 2026, Polymarket had the market "US October unemployment rate" open. It had 9 options: "3.8% or less", "3.9%", "4.0%" and so on up to "4.6% or more". The official figure comes out on Nov 6 and only one option pays 1 USD per share; the other 8 pay 0.

If you buy one share of each option, you know in advance that you will collect exactly 1 USD, whatever happens. So if buying all 9 cost less than 1 USD, you would earn the difference regardless of the outcome.

How much did it cost? Polymarket does not set prices: people do. Each one leaves orders such as "I sell 50 shares of '4.2%' at 0.34" or "I buy 30 at 0.32". The list of those waiting orders is the order book. From it come two prices per option:

  • the ask price: the cheapest price at which someone sells to you right now. For "4.2%" it was 0.34 USD;
  • the bid price: the highest price at which someone pays you right now. For "4.2%" it was 0.32 USD.

The names take the point of view of whoever left the order: you buy at someone else's ask.

Adding up the ask prices of the 9 options: 0.29 + 0.34 + 0.28 for the top three, plus 0.166 for the other six, gives 1.076 USD. You pay 1.076 to collect 1.00: you lose 7.6 cents per set.

It can also go the other way. Polymarket lets you create the complete set: you deposit 1 USD and it hands you one share of each option. If you sell all 9 at their bid prices, you collect 0.924: you also lose 7.6 cents. Confirmed

If either of those sums had landed on the good side, there would be profit regardless of the outcome. That is called arbitrage.

Where the numbers come from. Snapshot of all active markets, Oct 26, 2026, 03:08 UTC. The full table is in appendix A. This market charged no fee (rate 0).

Mechanism

A multi-option market (Polymarket calls them negRisk) promises that exactly one option wins. With that promise you can build a complete set: one share of each option. The set always pays 1 USD. That is why, at equilibrium, their prices add up to 1.

There are two ways for the sum to deviate in your favor:

  • Buy them all for less than 1: the sum of the ask prices is below 1.
  • Sell them all for more than 1: you create the set for 1 USD and the sum of the bid prices is above 1.

Between the bid and the ask there is always a distance, the spread. That is why, in a normal market, buying everything costs a bit more than 1 and selling everything yields a bit less. That margin is the payment to whoever leaves orders waiting. It is not an opportunity.

The Polymarket page shows a displayed price, the mid price or the last trade's. Adding displayed prices and finding 1.03 tells you nothing: it may be just the spread.

Another example, from chapter 6: the New York temperature markets have 11 degree ranges. Over 60 days, their history prices added up to 1.022 on average. It was margin, not opportunity.

Why it matters

It is the difference between Polyquack and almost everything out there. Articles and several tools add up displayed prices and announce opportunities that cannot be taken. Your first required criterion, "edge with real prices", exists precisely so we do not fall into that.

What we don't know

  • How often the sum of ask prices falls below 1, or the sum of bid prices rises above 1, in today's markets. A single snapshot does not say. Hypothesis: it happens rarely and lasts briefly (chapter 3).

Suppose a set that costs less than 1 does show up. How much do you keep in hand? That is chapter 2.

2What does it really cost to take an opportunity?

Short answer There are three costs: the fee paid by whoever buys at the book price (heavier when the price is near 0.50), the volume available at that price, and the time the money stays trapped until resolution. The "unemployment ≥ 6% / ≥ 7%" pair leaves 2.6% and traps the money for 3.2 months: about 10% a year.

Case

Another market from Oct 26: "How high will US unemployment go in 2026?". It is not multi-option: it is 5 yes-or-no questions ("does it reach at least 5.0%?", "5.5%?", "6.0%?", "7.0%?", "10%?"). The questions hold an ordering rule: if unemployment reaches 7%, it also reached 6%. We call this a ladder.

The prices at that moment contradicted the rule. "Reaches 6%" sold at 0.002, and someone paid 0.028 for "reaches 7%". That is, the market was saying 7% was more likely than 6%, which is impossible.

To take advantage, you buy two things:

  • Yes on "reaches 6%", at 0.002 USD: what the market sells cheap;
  • No on "reaches 7%", at 0.972 USD: the other face of what the market pays dearly for.

Why the No? Each question has Yes and No shares, and a Yes plus a No are always worth 1 together. If someone pays 0.028 for a 7% Yes, buying the No is equivalent to selling them that Yes: it costs you 1 − 0.028 = 0.972.

You pay 0.974 USD. How much do you collect? There are three scenarios:

If unemployment…Yes 6%No 7%You collect
does not reach 6%011
reaches 6% but not 7%112
reaches 7%101

Whatever happens, you collect at least 1 USD. You earn at least 1 − 0.974 = 0.026 USD per share, 2.6% of the payout. The middle scenario pays 2 USD: it is an extra prize if unemployment ends between 6% and 7%, but the math does not count on it. This market charged no fee. Confirmed

Now the volume. At those prices there were not infinite shares. Walking both books level by level, 433 shares fit at a cost below 1, for a total profit of 4.54 USD. That is less than 433 × 0.026 because the next levels of the book are more expensive.

And the time. The market closes on Feb 1, 2027: the money stays trapped for about 3.2 months. 2.6% in 3.2 months equals about 10% a year (2.6 × 12 / 3.2 = 9.75).

A deliberately small case. It is the best opportunity with a real book that the probe found. The 6 that looked better were phantom prices (chapter 5).

Mechanism

The fee. On Polymarket only whoever buys at the book price, the taker, pays. Whoever leaves an order waiting, the maker, pays 0 and also receives a rebate. The official formula, in words: shares × rate × price × (1 − price). The product price × (1 − price) is maximal at 0.50 and nearly zero near 0 or 1. Confirmed

An example from the documentation: 100 shares at 0.50 in Politics, with rate 0.04, pay 100 × 0.04 × 0.50 × 0.50 = 1.00 USD in fees, 2% of the amount invested. Rates go from 0 (Geopolitics) to 0.07 (Crypto). They changed six times between January and July 2026, and some recent weather markets charge 0. That is why the real fee is read from each market. Confirmed

The volume. An opportunity is worth as much as the shares available at that price. You have to walk the book level by level until the cost reaches 1. Also, each market has a minimum order size, normally 5 shares.

The time. The money is trapped from the moment you buy until the market resolves and pays out. If nobody disputes the resolution, payment arrives about 2 h after the result is proposed; if there is a dispute, 4 to 6 days. The profit has to be compared against what that money would earn elsewhere over the same time.

Resolution risk. "Safe" assumes the market resolves as its rules say. There have been disputed cases, such as one of about 7 M USD in Mar 2025, and the frequency figures are not comparable with each other Issue. Subtracting a risk cost per category is a pending option (DQ-007).

Why it matters

These three costs are criterion CR-001, "real net edge". A tool that ignores even one announces profits that do not exist. The evaluator that the brief asks for has to calculate all three every time.

What we don't know

  • Whether Polymarket's converter (chapter 3) charges anything: the documentation does not say. Issue
  • Other minor costs, such as deposits and withdrawals through intermediaries. Polymarket does not charge them; the intermediaries may.
  • How much your money earns in the alternative (the rate you compare against). Ask

We now know what an opportunity is and what it costs to take. Has anyone already measured how many there are?

3What has someone already measured, and what is left open?

Short answer IMDEA estimated about 40 M USD extracted in one year, without fees or an order book. Gebele measured with real 2026 books and with fees: the differences that close last seconds (median 16 s), but 58.7% last more than 50 minutes. What is left open is everything after May 2026, ladders, and whether it can be traded from home.

Case

IMDEA (Saguillo et al., 2025) reviewed Polymarket trades between Apr 2024 and Apr 2025 and estimated how much the accounts that exploited price differences took out. It adds up to 39.6 M USD, assuming 1 USD of profit per trade. They used the average price of each block's trades, not the order book. They did not subtract fees because none were charged back then. Confirmed

Gebele, Mutzel and Matthes (Aug 2026) did what IMDEA could not: they replayed full order books, update by update, on a sample of hours from Apr 14 to May 19, 2026. They measured violations with depth and fees, only in multi-option markets. Confirmed

They did not record the whole period: they chose individual hours and, in each one, replayed the book minute by minute. In those hours they observed 624 episodes, that is, stretches during which a difference stayed open after fees:

  • 366 (58.7%) lasted more than 50 minutes. They filled the whole observed hour, so it is unknown how much longer they lasted.
  • The other 258 were seen opening and closing. Of those, 253 were on the Yes side, with a median of 16.15 s, and 5 on the No side, with a median of 7.99 s. Both sides are explained below.

And on the money: the participants took out 1.118 M USD. Almost all of it, 1.086 M, came from the converter, explained below. Only 32,000 came from buying baskets (the complete set from chapter 1) and waiting for settlement. These are totals for the periods they studied, calculated from each account's trades.

The 40 M and the 1.1 M cannot be compared. They measure different periods, markets and methods. An earlier version of this analysis said "with real prices there is 35 times less": that was an error and the independent review flagged it.

Mechanism

An episode begins when the difference becomes positive after fees and ends at the first book update in which it stops being so. Its ending does not prove that someone exploited it: it can also close because someone moved an order.

In a multi-option market, each option has Yes and No shares. Polymarket's converter instantly exchanges 1 No of one option for 1 Yes of each of the others. It makes sense: "so-and-so does not win" is the same as "one of the others wins". With that, a difference can go in two directions (simplified):

  • No side: the Nos are cheap relative to the Yeses. You buy the Nos, convert them into Yeses and sell those Yeses immediately. There are few episodes and they are brief: someone takes them quickly.
  • Yes side: the Yeses are cheap. The reverse conversion does not exist, so the only way to collect is to buy all the Yeses (the basket) and wait for the market to resolve. There are many episodes and some last hours: taking them requires trapping money.

Why it matters

It changes what Polyquack can contribute. Measuring arbitrage in multi-option markets with real books has already been done. What we did not find published:

  • what happens after May 19, 2026. Since then, Polymarket raised the sports fee in July (from 0.03 to 0.05) and finished moving to its version 2, which changed the money used and the converter;
  • the ladders from chapter 2, which are standalone yes-or-no questions and not multi-option markets, so Gebele does not measure them;
  • whether anyone on a home connection can take anything.

What we don't know

  • Where Gebele got the 2026 books from; they cite a source [11] that we did not review. Hypothesis: a data vendor.
  • Whether their results stay the same after Polymarket's migration to its version 2 (Apr 28, 2026), which falls in the middle of their sample.

If half the opportunities last seconds, does it make sense to compete on speed?

Part II · The angles

4A · Can you win on speed from home?

Short answer Probably not, although your reaction time has not been measured. The opportunities that close on their own last seconds and Polymarket's engine is in London. The ones that last hours are almost always collected by waiting for settlement, and then speed no longer matters.

Case

Take Gebele's median: a Yes-side episode lasts 16 s. To take it from your home in Mexico you have to:

  1. receive the book update;
  2. detect that the sum fell below 1;
  3. send one order for each option in the market (in the chapter 1 case that would be 9). Each of those purchases is called a leg;
  4. have all of them fill before someone else takes them.

Polymarket's engine is on AWS, in London Strong hypothesis: its Chinese-language documentation says so and it was not seen in the English version. Polymarket offers to place servers next to it for those who verify their identity. The bots that live there receive and send earlier. Hypothesis: how much earlier was not measured.

And if you fill 8 legs and the ninth is no longer there, the set is left incomplete: collecting 1 USD is no longer guaranteed. If the option you missed is the one that wins, you lose almost everything you paid.

Your criterion CR-002 says "runs from home: needs no servers next to the exchange". That is why the server in London or Dublin (OP-020) was ruled out: it is the same idea as co-location.

Mechanism

The diagram puts on the same timeline how long an opportunity lasts and how long you would take to react. The scale grows from left to right: each tick is worth much more than the previous one. It has four rows:

  • Differences that close: the 258 episodes from chapter 3 that were seen opening and closing.
  • Long differences: the 366 that stayed open more than 50 minutes.
  • Your reaction from home: not measured.
  • Waiting for settlement: the time that passes if you collect a long Yes-side difference by buying the basket.

Why it matters

If A were an angle for making money, it would fail your required criterion "runs from home" on the short differences, and "real net edge" on the long ones (trapped capital). As a measurement it does have value: recording books costs 0 USD and nobody has published anything after May 2026 or on ladders.

What we don't know

  • How long an order really takes from your connection. It was not measured. Hypothesis: hundreds of milliseconds, not counting the time to decide.
  • How many of the long differences are on the No side, which is collected without waiting. In Gebele's sample, almost all were on the Yes side.

If speed is not the edge, is understanding? Ladders are corrected by reasoning, not by running.

5B · Do ladders make money?

Short answer The rule is precise: the 60 pairs in the sample were all valid. But in the Oct 26 snapshot, after removing phantom prices, the total usable profit was about 17 USD, as a ceiling. A single snapshot does not say how often they appear or how long they last.

Case

The "unemployment ≥ 6% / ≥ 7%" pair from chapter 2 came out of this probe. The probe downloaded all 21,823 active Polymarket events in one snapshot (Oct 26, 2026, 03:08 UTC). Then it looked for ladders: events whose markets only change a threshold ("above X") or a date ("before X").

In a ladder, the Yes of the hard market implies the Yes of the easy one. Reaching 7% implies having reached 6%. "Happens before June" implies "happens before December". If buying Yes on the easy one and No on the hard one costs less than 1, it is an opportunity, by the same three-scenario table from chapter 2.

The rule needed four versions. Each error taught something that an AI would also have to solve:

  • v1 treated "hit" as "go up". Stock events mix "hit (LOW)" (fall to) with "hit (HIGH)" (rise to), and dozens of false opportunities came out.
  • v2 separated LOW and HIGH, but "Trump's approval reaches 20%" means going down: it depends on today's value, which the rule does not know.
  • v3 accepted only explicit words ("or higher", "above") and discarded a bare "hit". It got the year wrong: some events close before some of their markets.
  • v4 takes the year from each market's close.
Precision. 60 pairs were drawn at random from the 13,776 and all 60 were valid implications. With 0 errors in 60, the true precision is at least 95% with 95% confidence (rule of three: 3 / 60 = 5% error at most). I reviewed them by reading the questions, not you, and the full rules of each market were not read Hypothesis. It does not measure how many ladders are left out.

Mechanism

The diagram follows the events through each filter, from the full snapshot to the money that could be taken.

The phantom prices deserve a comment. Gamma, Polymarket's markets API, reported a bid and an ask price for markets whose book did not exist (it answered "404, not found"). A scanner that trusts those prices announces 5% opportunities that nobody can take. The first version of this analysis fell for that and the independent review flagged it.

From 34 to 33: the pair on the valuation of the company Glean had only 0.36 shares, below the minimum of 5 per order. Each pair's fee was read from its own market; many charged 0.

Why it matters

B meets two criteria: it runs from home and costs 0 USD. For the third, net edge, the evidence from one snapshot is small: tens of dollars and months of trapped capital. As a portfolio piece it does contribute. The rule is precise, almost nobody has published it, and it shows the principle of not trusting a number without checking where it comes from.

What we don't know

  • How often they appear and how long they last: a single snapshot does not say. Books have to be recorded over time, the same thing angle A would do as a measurement. Issue
  • How many ladders the rule leaves out: coverage was not measured.
  • Ladders across different events on the same asset (for example, "BTC above X on day D" against "BTC touches X in October"). It is a new option (OP-046), not tested.

Both A and B look for prices that contradict each other. What if the edge came from knowing more than the market?

6C · Does a forecast beat the weather market?

Short answer The simple version does not: in New York and London, the market was wrong less often than the public forecast from one day earlier, and blending them did not improve on the market. A version with more information remains open (several models, same-day data, ML with years of history), but it would have to know something the market does not.

Case

Market: "Highest temperature in New York on Oct 22, 2026?". It has 11 ranges: "55 °F or lower", "56–57 °F", … , "74 °F or higher". It resolves with the LaGuardia airport station.

On Oct 21 at 16:00 UTC (noon in New York), one day earlier:

  • The public forecast from the European weather center (ECMWF, free via Open-Meteo) gave a high of 63.2 °F.
  • The model had learned from previous days that this forecast ran short by 0.64 °F on average. Its center ended up at 63.8 °F.
  • The typical forecast error on previous days was 2.23 °F. With that, the model spread the probability in a bell shape and gave 17.8% to the "66–67 °F" range.
  • The market gave 56.7% to that same range.

"66–67 °F" won. The model expected a colder day than there was: its favorite was "64–65 °F". The market already had the correct range as its favorite, something the one-day-earlier forecast did not say.

How it was scored. The Brier score adds up, for each range, the square of the difference between the probability given and what happened (1 if it won, 0 if not). The market: (1 − 0.567)² + 0.313² + 0.086² + … ≈ 0.29. The model: (1 − 0.178)² + 0.332² + 0.292² + 0.121² + … ≈ 0.89. Lower is better.

Mechanism

From the case to the 60 days. The probe did the same for each day from Aug to Oct 2026: 74 days in New York and 75 in London. The first 14 only served for the model to learn, so 60 and 61 were scored. Each day, the model learned the bias and the typical error only from previous days. This is called walk-forward: that way the model cannot cheat by seeing the future.

To give the Brier a scale: it goes from 0 (getting it right with 100%) to 2 (giving 100% to a range that did not occur). Spreading evenly across 11 ranges would give 0.91. The model (0.75) barely improves on that; the market (0.59) improves quite a bit more.

The "blend" column is the key test. If blending 80% of the price with 20% of the model improved on the price alone, the forecast would bring information the market does not have. The opposite happened: the blend got worse in both cities.

  • That the market beats the simple model: Confirmed.
  • That the forecast adds nothing: Hypothesis. With 60 days a small contribution cannot be ruled out. In New York, the blend got worse by 0.015, about 2.5 times its standard error (0.006), so it is unlikely to be luck. In London it got worse by 0.009, only 1.4 times its standard error: it may be noise.

This "lower is better" measures information, not money: the probe used history prices, not executable prices.

Why it matters

C was the angle with the most ML. The probe rules out the naive version, not the whole angle. To beat the market, a model would have to use something the market does not use:

  • ensemble forecasts: dozens of runs of the same model with small variations, which say how certain the prediction is;
  • high-resolution models, which look at a smaller area in more detail;
  • the station's observations during the same day;
  • years of history to train on.

Any of those versions starts at a disadvantage. In New York, the center of the market's bet was off from the real high by 1.15 °F on average, and the uncorrected forecast by 1.90 °F (74 days). In London it was 0.66 °C against 1.14 °C. The 2.23 °F in the case is something else: the spread the model used to draw its bell that day.

Also, C seeks an information edge, not arbitrage. The brief approved arbitrage, contradictions and calibration. If C goes ahead, it is a scope expansion that you have to approve. Ask

Limits of the probe. It used history prices every 3 h, not executable prices: it measures information, not net edge. Midway through the period, in both cities, the source used to resolve changed: from Weather Underground to NOAA. The model learned its initial bias with the old source; how much that distorts was not measured. The "one day earlier" forecast came, for the peak hours, from runs published after the market snapshot. That favors the model, and it still lost.

What we don't know

  • Whether a version with ensembles, same-day observations or ML beats the market. It was not tested.
  • Whether the 0 fee on recent weather markets, since Oct 19, 2026, holds. It was only seen on weather markets. Hypothesis: it is a new change.

If the public forecast is already in the price, is there another source of information that the prediction market does not use well?

7D and calibration · Is there an edge in another market or in a bias?

Short answer Crypto options give a fair price, and a paper already found deviations in the "touch X" bets. On calibration, one study sees undervalued favorites at long horizons and another sees no edge. Nobody has measured the net profit of either, and neither was probed.

Case: D, crypto options

Polymarket has markets on the BTC price of two types:

  • "Above X": only the price on one date matters, for example "BTC above 120,000 on Dec 31?".
  • "Touch X?": it is enough for the price to pass through X at some point in the period, even if it then moves away.

On Deribit, a crypto derivatives exchange much larger and used by professionals Hypothesis, financial options on BTC are traded. With a standard mathematical model, you can back out from their prices the probability that market assigns to each case. If it does not match Polymarket's, one of the two is wrong.

What is already known Confirmed from the abstract of a paper comparing the two markets:

  • The "above X" bets track the options well.
  • The "touch X?" or "falls to X?" ones deviate. More at market opening, near expiry and on weekends.

That they deviate does not mean you can win: fees, spread and trapped capital still have to be subtracted, and nobody has done it.

Why it was not probed. It passes your criteria: free data and runs from home. It was left out because it is already studied, and novelty is not one of your criteria. It remains a valid option.

Case: calibration

A market is calibrated if, of all the contracts quoted at 70 cents, about 70% come true. Two studies disagree:

  • Le (2026), with trades through Dec 2025: in politics, favorites are worth less than they should be, and more so at long horizons. A 70-cent contract at one month equals about 75% in reality. It does not subtract fees. Confirmed
  • Song, with 2,975 markets: finds no such bias, and correcting for it out of sample improved nothing. It is an analysis published on GitHub, without peer review: the numbers are from its text, but nobody else has reviewed the method.
The bias and time. A 70 contract being worth 75 gives 5 cents, but over a month or more. It is the same trapped-capital problem from chapter 2.

Mechanism

Both are information edges:

  • D brings in a fair price from a more professional market.
  • Calibration exploits a systematic error of the people who trade on Polymarket.

Neither needs speed, so both pass "runs from home". Both depend on the same thing: that the difference survives the fee, the spread and the waiting time. Nobody has shown it.

Why it matters

D is the classic most "quant" angle and has a solid mathematical base. Measuring the net edge of calibration, after fee, spread and time, is something no study does. It would also use the same data as the rest of Polyquack. Neither has been ruled out with evidence.

What we don't know

  • Whether you can trade on Deribit from Mexico, if you wanted to hedge. It was not checked.
  • Whether Le's bias persists in 2026 and whether it survives fees.
  • D, like C, expands the scope of the brief. Ask

D uses the prices of another market to judge Polymarket. What if the other market were inside Polymarket, and what was compared were not the price but what is charged for holding a position?

8E · Can you collect the funding spread between platforms?

Short answer Polymarket has had perpetual futures since Sep 2026, and its funding rate was lower than Hyperliquid's on 36 of 38 cryptos. Buying on one and selling on the other collects that spread without betting on the price. Out of sample it left +8.7% a year on average, with wide dispersion and unmeasured risks.

Case

Since Sep 3, 2026, Polymarket has perpetual futures (Perps). They are contracts that follow the price of an asset, such as ETHFI, a mid-sized crypto, and never expire.

So the contract price does not drift from the real price, a funding rate is charged every hour:

  • if the contract trades above the real price, those who bought (longs) pay those who sold (shorts);
  • if it trades below, the shorts pay.

Hyperliquid, another perpetuals platform, does the same.

Between Sep 3 and Oct 26, 2026, with ETHFI:

  • On Polymarket, the average rate was −24.1% a year: the shorts paid the longs.
  • On Hyperliquid it was +13.7% a year: the longs paid the shorts.

If you buy ETHFI on Polymarket and sell the same amount on Hyperliquid, you collect on both platforms. We call this variant E1. What you gain or lose from the ETHFI price cancels out, because you are long on one and short on the other. What is left is the sum of the two rates: 24.1 + 13.7 = 37.8% a year, before fees.

Where the numbers come from. Public hourly funding history from the two platforms. The 38 cryptos that are on both. Scripts in docs/discovery/spikes/DQ-E-funding/.

Mechanism

From the case to the pattern. ETHFI is not the exception:

  • On 36 of the 38 cryptos that are on both platforms, Polymarket's funding was lower than Hyperliquid's. The average difference is 12.5% a year. Confirmed for these two months.
  • Why it happens is not clear. What is seen: on Hyperliquid, the rate almost always stayed at its normal level (about 11% a year) or above. On Polymarket, several altcoins traded below the real price, with more people selling than buying, and that made their rate negative. It may be something specific to a new platform with few participants. Hypothesis

From the pattern to the rule. A strategy that cannot see the future was simulated:

  • each day it looks at the difference over the previous 24 h;
  • if it exceeds a threshold (8.8% a year), it holds for 24 h the position that collects, whichever it is: long on Polymarket and short on Hyperliquid, or the reverse. The threshold avoids opening and closing for small differences that do not pay the fee. It was chosen by looking at the same data, and that is a weakness;
  • it pays the taker fee on both legs when opening and closing: 0.17% of the value of one leg for each full round trip, already subtracted from the net. The 37.8% in the case is gross, without that subtraction.

Over the 38 coins and the two full months, it left +2.6% a year on average, positive on 17.

The test that matters. So as not to keep only the coins that came out well by luck, the 10 with the largest difference in the first half (Sep 3 to 29) were chosen and measured only in the second (Sep 29 to Oct 26):

  • average: +8.7% a year, against +4.3% for all coins in that same second half (it differs from the +2.6% because it is a different period);
  • enormous dispersion, from −9.9% to +34.3%;
  • three went above +30% (ETHFI, ARB, KAITO) and three lost.
Return on your money. The percentages are on the value of each leg. Each leg needs its collateral (margin): with 2x leverage, a 1,000 USD leg requires 500, and the two together require 1,000. So the return on your money stays equal to these percentages. With 4x you put up half and the return doubles. But if the price moves a bit less than 25% against one leg, the platform closes it (liquidates), and the other is left unhedged.

Why it matters

It is the first angle that left positive net profit on data it did not use to choose. It passes your two required criteria:

  • it runs from home, because it is collected by the hour and not by the second;
  • the profit was measured with the actual rates charged and with fees.

It also does not suffer from the problem in chapter 2: funding is collected every hour, not at the end, so the money is not trapped waiting for a resolution. What does get immobilized is the collateral of the two legs.

The data is free. You can trade from Mexico: it is not on Perps' list of blocked countries. Confirmed for the platform; the Mexican legal framework was not reviewed.

There is a second variant, E2, not probed: comparing or hedging the price prediction markets ("BTC above X?") with the BTC perp, inside the same platform. It is like D, but without leaving Polymarket.

Like C and D, E is no longer a prediction market. If it goes ahead, it expands the brief. Ask

Depth in an Oct 26 snapshot. Within 0.2% of the price there was, for example, 125,000 USD of asks on Polymarket for ETHFI, but only 5,000 of bids on Hyperliquid. For sizes of thousands of USD it is enough; the small side is usually Hyperliquid.

What we don't know

  • Basis risk: how far the two prices diverge while the position is open. In the Oct 26 snapshot, Polymarket quoted between 0.03% and 0.12% below Hyperliquid. It was not measured over time. Issue
  • Whether it lasts: the platform is two months old. If more people arrive, the difference may close, and beta-stage fees may change.
  • Slippage and liquidations: not modeled.
  • Legality in Mexico of leveraged derivatives: not reviewed.

Glossary

Option (of a market)
One of the possible outcomes. On resolution, the winner pays 1 USD per share and the others 0.
Multi-option market (negRisk)
A market whose options exclude each other: exactly one wins. Polymarket calls it negRisk.
Complete set (basket)
One share of each option of a multi-option market. It always pays 1 USD.
Order book
The list of waiting buy and sell orders, with their price and quantity.
Executable price (ask and bid price)
The best price at which you can really buy right now (ask price) or sell right now (bid price).
Displayed price
The one the page shows: the mid price or the last trade's. It is not useful for deciding on a trade.
Spread
The distance between the ask price and the bid price. It is the payment to whoever leaves orders waiting.
Taker and maker
Taker: whoever buys or sells at the book price, immediately; pays a fee. Maker: whoever leaves an order waiting; pays nothing and receives a rebate.
Arbitrage
A combination of buys and sells that leaves a profit whatever happens with the outcome.
Converter
A Polymarket tool that exchanges 1 No of one option for 1 Yes of each of the others, in multi-option markets.
Leg
Each individual buy or sell within a trade that combines several. A set of 9 options has 9 legs.
Perpetual future (perp)
A contract that follows the price of an asset and never expires. It can be bought (long) or sold (short) with leverage.
Funding rate
A payment that, every hour, one side of the perpetual makes to the other to keep its price tied to the real price. If the perp is expensive, the longs pay; if it is cheap, the shorts.
Ladder
A group of markets that only change a threshold or a date. The Yes of the hard one implies the Yes of the easy one.
Phantom price
A price that the markets API reports for a market with no real book. It cannot be executed.
Episode
The time a price difference stays positive after fees, from when it opens to when it closes.
Brier score
Measures the error of a probability: for each possible outcome, it adds the square of the difference between the probability given and what happened. Lower is better.
Walk-forward
Evaluating a model by letting it learn only from data prior to each day it predicts, so that it does not see the future.
Calibration
How closely the probabilities in prices match the real frequency with which they come true.
Financial options
Contracts that give the right to buy or sell an asset at a price on a date. Their prices imply probabilities about where the asset will be.
